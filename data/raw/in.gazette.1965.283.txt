by any other person.

submit the case for the decision of the Central Government together 
with the record of the proceedings held by him and a report con- 
taining his recommendations on the objections. 

(3) For the purposes of this section, a person shall be deemed to be inte- 

rested in land who would be entitled to claim an interest in com- 
pensation if the land or any rights in or over such land were acquir- 
ed under this Act.”

Item 22— Flight Duty Time, Flight Time and Rest Periods for Pilots 
(a) Definitions: 

(i) Flight Duty Time. — Tire total time commencing from the time of report- 
ing at the airport and ending with the termination of a flight or a series of flights 
(chocks on plus 15 minutes), unless broken by a period of rest as stipulated in 
para e(ii) hereunder: — 

(ii) Flight Time. — The total time from the moment an aircraft first taxies out 
under its own power for the purpose of take-off to the moment it comes to rest 
at the end of the flight. Flight time is synonymous with “Block to Block Time” 
or “Chock to Chock Time”. 

(iii) Total hours of duty. — The period during which a pilot is performing any 
function assigned by the Corporation. 

(iv) Rest Period. — The period during which a Pilot is not assigned any func- 
tion by the Corporation. 

(v) Clear Rest. — The period commencing from the time a room is allotted 
for occupation at the hotel up to the call time, which would normally be one 
hour before pick-up time. 

(vi) Layover. — The period spent between “chocks on" and “chocks off” at an 
outatation. 

(vii) Standby crew. — A pilot who is required to standby during a specified 
period, fully prepared and ready to undertake a flight when called upon, 

(vill) Crew Base. — A place where a pilot is posted either on a temporary 
or permanent basis. The word “Base" used signifies “Crew Base”. 

(ix) Crew Change Station. — Where a layover or scheduled change of crew 
takes place.

2. The reference was registered here as industrial dispute No. 58/1964. The

statement was received only on 10th of April 1965 and the counter by the employ-

referred. I reached the conclusion that they do. I will state here the said terms. 
They are as follows: — 

‘‘1, Sri K. Rajam will be promoted to Category VII as Blacksmith with 
effect from 1st November 1963. 

2. The arrears of wages due as per the settlement will be paid to Sri 

Kamari Rajam within a period of 2 months. 

3. The dispute stands fully and finally settled; and 

4. There will be no costs.” 

4. It will be seen that the employer has made K, Rajam a blacksmith and 
has allowed him the wages of not only of Category VI but of Category VII. It 
will be noted that under the Coal Award blacksmiths are placed in three cate- 
gories, Category V, VI and VII. It is evident, therefore, that he has been given 
the highest wages of a blacksmith under the Coal Award, The claim was that 
he was working for the past 5 years as such, but, the compromise reached allows 
him the wages" from 1st November 1963 i.e., for about 2 years. As the parties 
have agreed to this much retrospective effect, I allow it. The issues are fully 
answered and this Award is passed in accordance with the terms of the settle- 
ment.

On Merits 

8. The principles governing jurisdiction of an industrial tribunal dealing with 
an industrial dispute have now been set at rest by several decisions of the Supreme 
Court. I will, however, refer to one of such decisions on this point and that is 
the case of Tata Oil Mills Co. Ltd. Vs. Their Workmen, (1963) 6 F.L.R. 257, in 
which it has been observed as follows: 

“The true legal position about the jurisdiction of the Tribunal in dealing 
with an industrial dispute of this type is now well settled. If it is 
shown that the employer has held a proper enquiry, the Tribunal 
would not be entitled to consider the propriety or the correctness of 
the conclusions reached by the Enquiry Officer at such enquiry. The 
Tribunal will be entitled to consider the said conclusions if they 
appear to be perverse or if the Tribunal is satisfied that the enquiry 
was unfair, or, either the proceedings or the final order passed 
against the workman amounted to victimisation or adoption of an 
unfair labour practice. If none of these facts is established, then the 
findings made by the Enquiry Officer in the domestic enquiry should 
be taken to be binding against the employee.” 

9. Bearing in mind the above limitations of its jurisdiction In dealing with an 
industrial dispute by an Industrial Tribunal, I will not proceed to deal with the 
domestic enquiry and the objections thereto raised, on behalf of the workman. 

The first objection raised by Shrl Chanda was that the finding of the Enquiry 
Officer was perverse, because the Enquiry Officer did not probe Into the cause of 
the dismissal of the concerned workman, nor did he consider that no one could 
bring such a Sleeper from underground and further that he should not have dis- 
believed the two defence witnesses on the ground that they did not remember the 
date, when they accompanied the concerned workman and when they found him 
carrying no Sleeper. I am afraid, there Is no substance In this objection. The 
Enquiry Officer, as appears from his report. Ext. M-12, has in clear terms stated 
that “on the face of the convincing evidence submitted by Shrl Subedar Mishra, 
and Shri S. P. Banerjee. I feel satisfied that Shri Mazld Mia Is guilty of the charge 
brought against him.” It cannot, therefore, be said that hlg finding that the con- 
cerned workman was guilty is not supported by any evidence or his entirely 
opposed to the whole body of the evidence adduced before him. 

The Enquiry Officer has considered the evidence of the two defence witnesses 
and It cannot be said that he was not justified in not relying on their evidence, 
when they could not remember the date, when they accompanied the accused In 
the night after ‘B’ shift duty, when he had no sleeper with him. The evidence 
given by the defence witnesses is vague and indefinite, and, therefore, the evidence 
of these two defence witnesses did not negative the evidence given by the eye 
witnesses. 

The contention that no one could bring the Sleeper from the underground and, 
therefore, the prosecution story was improbable was not placed before the Enquiry 
Officer, otherwise there is no reason why he should not have dealt with It. In 
my opinion, It deserves no attention also because this point was not brought out 
in the evidence at. the enquiry at all nor was it put to any witness in his connection. 
It may be mentioned that neither Shrl Subedar Mishra (Ext, M-5), nor Shrl S. P- 
Banerjee (Ext. M-6) were cross-examined by the workman concerned at all, nor 
did the workman put to Shri Subedar Mishra, as now alleged, that there was 
enmity with him, The proper persons to be asked on the question whether it was 
probable to bring the Sleeper from underground, were the two witnesses of the 
management, but not a single question was put to them, and, as a matter of fact, 
as stated above they were not cross-examined by the workman, who declined to 
cross-examine them. Even the two defence witnesses could have been asked on 
this point, but they were not asked, The workman was given fullest opportunity 
to cross-examine the two witnesses, mentioned above, who were examined on 
behalf of the Company, but the workman did not cross-examine them and he

examlned hla two witnesses, who were cross-examined by the Enquiry Officer. It 
cannot, therefore, be said either that the enquiry was not fair or that the con- 
cerned workman was not given the fullest opportunity at the enquiry to cross- 
examine the two witnesses of the Company and to defend himself, 


The Supreme Court in the case of Hamdard Daivakhana Wakf and its work- 
men (1983) 6 F.L.R. 86, has observed that Industrial Tribunals would be justified 
in characterising the finding recorded in the domestic enquiry as perverse only 
if it is shown that such a finding is not supported by any evidence or is entirely 
opposed to the whole body of the evidence adduced before him. Judging in this 
light it is clear that the finding of the Enquiry Officer is not at all perverse as it 
is fully supported by the evidence on record. 

10. Another objection, taken by Shri Chanda, was that the statement, which 
the concerned workman is alleged to have made on 6th April 1963, and which was 
recorded by the Assistant Manager (Ext, M), was relied upon by the Enquiry 
Officer, without supplying its copy to the concerned workman and without draw- 
ing the attention of the concerned workman to it. In my opinion, this objection 
also has no merit. This statement Ext. M was alleged to have been made by the 
concerned workman on 6th April 1963 and his thumb impression appears below 
his statement (Ext. M). In his reply Ext. M-2 to the charge-sheet Ext, M, he 
does not deny his statement (Ext. M) at all, nor does he say, as it is argued, that 
the statement was a prepared statement and he was only asked to put his signature 
on it. A look at this statement shows that it could not be a prepared statement, 
although I find that there are two cuttings in the statement; At first it was men- 
tioned 'one chaprasi’ Shri Subedar Singh, but later, the word ‘chaprasi’ was cut 
out and in his place ‘ Havildar ’ was mentioned and the word ‘Singh’ was cut out 
from the name of Shri Subedar and ‘Mtshra’ was added and at the top and 
bottom the word ‘Trammer’ was clut and the word ‘Clipman’ lias been put, but 
otherwise there is no cutting any where and the thumb impression is very clear 
which appears at the bottom of the statement. The concerned workman was not 
examined before the Tribunal. No doubt, in his explanation (Ext. M-2) to the 
chargesheet, the workman denied the charge and said that he had been Implicated 
due to enmity b(y the Havildar, but there is no mention, as stated, to his earlier 
statement, nor, did he deny at all his earlier statement, Subsequently, when he 
was examined at the enquiry on 26th April 1963, as it will appear from Ext. M-7, 
there also he did not deny his statement (Ext. M), nor did he say anything about 
It in his statement, which was open to him to do. Shri Subedar Mishra has 
stated in his evidence (Ext. M-5) that Shri S. P. Banerjee took down the work- 
man’s statement and also took charge of the sleeper. These statements were 
against the concerned workman but in spite of that, the concerned workman, 
although present at the enquiry, and although these statements were made in his 
presence, he declined to cross-examine him, as will appear from Ext. M-5. Then 
again, Shri S. P, Banerjee, Assistant Manager, stated (Ext. M-6) that he took 
down the statement of Shri Mazid Mia on the spot hi which he admitted to have 
taken the Sleeper for the purpose of making th<> legs of the cot. This statement 
was made in presence of the workman, still he declined to challenge its truth and 
to cross-examine this witness. In view of these circumstances, it cannot be said 
that a copy of the statement (Ext. M). taken in presence of the concerned work- 
man, bearing his thumb impression, should have been supplied to him and it 
should have been put to the concerned workman, when he did not deny the same 
at any stage of the enquiry, either In his reply to the chargesheet or at the 
enquiry and that it should not have been referred to for any purpose whatsoever 
by the Enquiry Officer in his enquiry report (Ext. M-12). I may add that no 
doubt the Enquiry Officer has referred to that statement of the workman recorded 
by Shri S. P. Banerjee, on 6th April 1963 and thereafter mentioned four facts, 
which emerge according to him, but all these four facts are proved from the 
evidence of these two witnesses themselves. But his finding, that the concerned 
workman is guilty of the charge, is based only on the evidence of Shri Subedar 
Mishra and Shri S. P. Banerjee as it will appear from the last paragraph of his 
report. For these reasons, In my opinion, the enquiry report is not vitiated at all 
and the enquiry report does not suffer from any infirmity. 

11. I, therefore, hold that the enquiry was fair and it does not suffer from any 
infirmity and the fact that the Enquiry Officer, has referred to the concerned 
workman’s statement (Ext. M), has not at all prejudiced him, when he did not 
deny the same at any stage of the proceeding, and. therefore, principles of natural 
justice have not been violated.

Issue No, 1 

The plea of the Bank, which has given rise to this Issue, is that the Reference 
itself is invalid because it assumes that Shri S. P. Hand a was a permanent 
employe-e and that his services were subsequently terminated. This plea 
hus obviously no force. The case of the workmen before the Conciliation 
Officer was that although the Bank officials had said that tire appointment 
of Mr, S. P. Handa was purely temporary the post against which the said appoint- 
ment had been made was a permanent one as it existed before the 
appointment and continued to exist even after the termination of Mr. Handa’s 
services. The plea taken by the workmen was that the appointment was not 
infact on temporary basis but was wrongly shown to he so by the Bank. The 
reference has, however, left the matter open for decision by this Tribunal 
whether Mr- S. P. Handa should be treated as a temporary employee or should 
be treated otherwise. The reference envisages a decision by the Tribunal on 
two points which are: 

1. Whether the Bank was justified in treating Mr, S, P. Handa as a tem- 

porary employee? and 

2. Whether the Bank was justified in terminating his services with effect 

from 31st May 1962? 

In fact I allowed the Bank to prove that Mr. S, P. Handa was a temporary 
employee and on the request of the Bank I framed Issue No. 2 which is specific 
on this point. The management has been wholly unable to convince me that 
the reference is in any way invalid. 

Issues Nos. 2 and 3. 

These two issues can be conveniently dealt with together and I shall deal 
with them so. The ease of the workmen is that at the time when a workman 
seeks a job he is generally out of job and is feeling helpless. He does not mind 
accepting a letter of appointment in Which it is said that the job is a purely 
temporary one and that his services would be liable to be terminated without 
any notice or without payment of any salary in lieu thereof. The fact that the 
appointment letter contains such a clause is by itself not conclusive evidence 
of the fact that the appointment was purely a temporary one. It is contended 
on their behalf that the Tribunal has to apply its mind to all the circumstances 
of the case and to the evidence led before it to find out whether the intention 
of the parties was that the employee should be treated as on purely temporary 
basis. My attention has been drawn to the fact that there was a person previ- 
ously on this job and that the job did Hot end on 31st May 1962 when the ser- 
vices of Shri S. P. Handa were dispensed with. The next argument of the work- 
men is that the appointment of Mr. S. P. Handa was really on probation basis 
and that If his work was found satisfactory he was to be confirmed. Their 
further case is that according to the All India Industrial Tribunal (Bank Dis- 
putes) award popularly known as Sastry award any probationer who has worked 
for six months must be deemed to have been automatically confirmed unless be 
has been informed to the contrary. 

The contention of the Bank on the other hand is that Mr. S. P. Handa was 
purely a temporary employee and that the Bank was justified in terminating 
his services at any time it liked. In my opinion it Is not necessary to record 
any considered judgment in this case on the point as to whether Mr. Han da’ a 
appointment was a purely temporary one or as to whether he was a probationer 
because I have no doubt' that even on the assumption that Mr. S- P. Handa was 
originally appointed as a purely temporary employee, the workmen must suc-

S.O. 2676. — In exercise of the powers conferred by section 157 of the Customs 
Act, 1962 (52 of 1962), the Central Board of Excise and Customs hereby makes 
the following regulations, namely:— 

1. Short title. — These regulations may be called the Bonded Aircraft Stores 
(Procedure) Regulations, 1965. 

2 . Definitions. — In these regulations— 

(a) “Act” means the Customs Act, 1962 (52 of 1962), 

(b) “Form” means a Form specified in the Schedule to these regulations. 

;i. Warehousing of goods for use as stores. — (1) Whereas any imported goods tor 
use in a foreign-going aircraft are to be entered for warehousing under section 85 
of tho Act, an application in Form I shall be made to the Assistant Collector of 
Customs. 

(2) Every such application shall be deemed to be the bill of entry in relation to 
the goods specified in that application for the purpose of section 46 of the Act. 

(3) On receipt of an application under sub-regulation (1), the Assistant Collector 
of Customs may permit the goods specified in that application to be warehoused 
without the goods being assessed to duty. 

4. Clearance of warehoused goods for supply as stores iu a foreign-going air- 
craft. — (1) Where goods permitted to be warehoused under sub-regulation (3) of 
regulation 3 are to be cleared for use as stores in a foreign-going aircraft, an 
application shall be made to the Assistant Collector of Customs in Form II. 

(2) Every such application shall be deemed to be the shipping bill in relation to 
the goods specified in that application for the purpose of section 50 of the Act. 

(3) On receipt of an application under sub-regulation (1), the Assistant Collector 
of Customs may permit the clearance of the warehoused goods specified in that 
application for being taken on board the foreign-going aircraft as stores In accord- 
ance with the provisions of section 69 of the Act as applied to stores by section 88 
of the said Act.
